01

The copyright question, settled

The instinct to rewrite everything comes from a reasonable place — wanting to own the product outright. Here is what the law actually provides, and where ownership really lives.

The licence is the permission

Nothing needs recoding

Apache-2.0, MIT, BSD and MPL are grants of copyright permission — written precisely so a commercial product can use, modify, combine and sell the software. That is their entire function. PostgreSQL in your product is not a copyright problem; it is the licence working as intended. Every major legal-tech platform ships this way.

The trap avoided

A rewrite adds risk, not safety

Re-implementing a project while working from its code produces a derivative work — the exact thing copyright restricts — while using it under licence is unambiguous and clean. A rewrite of thirty years of PostgreSQL or a decade of Tesseract would also simply be worse software: the battle-testing is the value. The compliant path is also the cheaper, safer, better one.

The actual obligations

Four small duties

Keep copyright notices intact. Ship the licence texts (an “Open Source Notices” screen in-app, like every product you own). Run AGPL components as isolated, unmodified services — already Rule 2 of the Build Sheet. Never use their names or logos to brand ours. That is the entire compliance burden, and CI enforces it: an SBOM per release via Syft, licences scanned by ScanCode, both open source themselves.

Where ownership lives

What is 100% ours

Everything differentiating: the 64-jurisdiction rules data, the citation gate, the trust ledger, the kernel schemas, every room's logic, the templates, the UI, the brand. That is the code we write, the copyright the company owns, and the asset a buyer would pay for. “Better than the original” happens here — in the layer the originals never had — not by re-typing engines.

02

One product, assembled

“Pull it all into one software” is exactly right — and it is packaging, not rewriting. The user sees one application; the components run inside it the way an engine block holds parts from forty suppliers.

Assembly

Pinned, built, sealed

Every component builds from a pinned, hash-verified source into our container images. No runtime downloads, no phoning home, no telemetry out. A release is a sealed set of images that installs as one product — cloud cell or the on-premise bundle, identical bits.

Surface

One face, ours

The 28 rooms are one interface, one login, one design language. Collabora edits inside our chrome; OpenSearch answers behind our API; nothing third-party-branded ever faces a lawyer or a client. The notices screen credits the components; the product is ours.

Improvement

Better, where better counts

Genuine improvements to a component — a faster OCR path, a fixed edge case — are contributed upstream where strategic and patched locally where not. What we never do is fork thirty projects into private copies we then maintain alone forever; that is how platforms die.

03

The front door

Closed to the public, by design. There is no landing page, no pricing page, no product tour, no signup button — the domain answers with a sign-in and says nothing else. Cinamate ends its own page behind a restricted-access gate; here the gate is the entire public face.

Surface

Nothing to browse

The apex serves the login and only the login. No marketing routes exist in the codebase to secure or to leak; robots.txt disallows everything, no sitemap is published, and search engines have nothing to index. What the product does, who uses it, and what it costs are conversations, not web pages.

Accounts

Provisioned, never self-served

There is no signup flow — not hidden, but absent from the code. A firm administrator provisions each account; invitations are single-use, expire in hours, bind to the invited address, and end in passkey enrollment, so the first credential is phishing-resistant from day one. Client portal access is granted per matter, the same way.

Discretion

A door that says nothing

The login confirms nothing to anyone. Unknown account and wrong credential return the same response in the same time; there is no “welcome back” name, no reset flow that reveals whether an address is registered, no version banner, no branded error page naming the stack. Attempts are rate-limited and logged to the audit chain.

Narrower still

Optional tighter doors

Per firm, the door can narrow further: IP allowlists for office and VPN ranges, device-bound sessions, mTLS client certificates for administrators, and — on a dedicated cell — access only over the firm's WireGuard link, so the login is not reachable from the public internet at all.

05

The key hierarchy

Four levels, so that keys can be held by the right party, rotated without re-encrypting the world, and destroyed matter by matter. Standard, audited primitives only — AES-256-GCM and XChaCha20-Poly1305 via libsodium — never home-rolled cryptography, for the same reason we don't rewrite PostgreSQL.

Root to row

Levels K1–K4 Each level wraps the one below it; unwraps are logged; nothing decrypts silently.
K1

Root of trust Ceremony

OpenBao per cell, hardware-backed

Each residency cell runs its own OpenBao (MPL-2.0) with the unseal material split five ways, three required — no single person can open it. The root never leaves the cell, never crosses the border, and every operation it performs lands in the audit chain.

Rotation
Annual ceremony, or on any officer departure
K2

Tenant key Custody choice

One KEK per firm — and the firm can hold it

Every firm gets its own key-encryption key. In the default model we custody it inside the cell's vault; under BYOK the firm's own vault holds it and our platform requests unwraps per session — which means the firm can revoke our access to their entire practice, unilaterally, at any moment. That is the custody a managing partner can describe to a client without hedging.

Rotation
Re-wrap of matter keys only — minutes, not a re-encryption of content
K3

Matter key

One DEK per matter — the wall and the shredder

Each matter's content encrypts under its own key, wrapped by the tenant KEK. An ethical screen is therefore cryptographic as well as logical: a screened lawyer's sessions can never obtain the unwrap. And when the retention schedule says destroy, destroying this one key renders the matter unreadable in the database, the object store, every replica and every backup simultaneously — the Closing Room's destruction certificate describes a mathematical fact.

Scope
Documents, facts, narratives, transcripts, embeddings — all matter content
K4

Working keys

Per-object, per-field, per-message

Beneath the matter key: a unique data key per blob in the object store (envelope encryption); authenticated field-level sealing on the most sensitive columns — privileged narratives, trust ledger detail — via libsodium; and end-to-end sealed messaging in the client portal, where the client–lawyer channel encrypts on the devices and the server relays what it cannot read.

In transit
TLS 1.3 outside, mTLS between every internal service; WireGuard for the on-premise site link
At rest
Encrypted volumes underneath everything, as the floor, not the story
06

Who holds the keys

Three custody models on one codebase. The honest engineering tradeoff, stated plainly: content the server can never read is also content the server cannot search, cite-check, or run models over. So the design encrypts everything, and lets the firm choose who custodies the keys — rather than pretending full zero-knowledge and a working Research Desk can coexist.

Platform custody

Default

Keys live in the cell's vault, hardware-backed, unwrap-logged, staff-inaccessible by policy and by break-glass alarm. All 28 rooms fully functional. The right answer for most firms.

BYOK

The firm's tenant key sits in the firm's own vault — their OpenBao, their KMS. We request unwraps; they can watch every request and cut us off entirely, at will. Full functionality while trust holds; provable custody the day it's questioned. The model to offer a top Toronto litigator.

Built for Ontario

The partner practises where solicitor-client privilege is guarded as jealously as anywhere in the common-law world, and where the regulator has opinions about technology. The architecture answers each obligation specifically.

Residency

The Canadian cell

A Canadian matter's rows, blobs, embeddings, keys, logs and backups live and stay in the Canadian cell — storage, processing, and inference included. No US transit, no cross-border replication, no exception for convenience. For clients under public-sector or health rules where residency is mandatory, this is the difference between eligible and not.

The regulator

LSO obligations, answered

The Law Society expects technological competence, supervised delegation, and confidentiality that survives the tools a firm chooses. The platform gives the firm its answers in writing: the encryption architecture, the audit chain, the provenance record on every generated document, and the human-signature gate that keeps the lawyer — not the software — as the professional actor.

Breach duties

Evidence before apology

PIPEDA's real-risk-of-significant-harm reporting turns on knowing what was actually exposed. Because access is key-mediated and unwrap-logged, a suspected incident produces a precise blast radius — which matters, which documents, which minutes — instead of a letter that says “may have included.”

Honesty

What encryption cannot do

Privilege is a legal doctrine; encryption protects the confidentiality that privilege presumes, and no vendor should claim more. Encryption also does not stop an authorised user misusing what they may lawfully see — that is walls, audit and supervision — and it does not defend an unlocked endpoint. The vault is one wall of the practice, built properly; it is not the practice.
